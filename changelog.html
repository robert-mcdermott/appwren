Changelog

AppWren follows semantic versioning. Each wave of the roadmap is a release. Before 1.0, a release may contain breaking changes; they are listed under Breaking.

0.6.0 (2026-11-27)#

Wave 6, Insight: the record read back as a whole, for leadership. Two portfolio snapshots side by side say what moved between them, and an application's scores are shown over time; shares of the portfolio name the costliest and least used applications without feeding any score; a leadership report for a period is worked out once and kept whole, with a digest that shows it is unchanged; the portfolio, and any application, can be seen as it stood at the end of a chosen day, from the history; and a scenario shows what a set of changes would save, whom it would affect, and what would stop, before anyone commits to it.

Added#

  • Trends. Reports → Trends puts two portfolio snapshots side by side, or one and today: the figures each held, the applications whose recommendation changed, whose scores rose or fell by half a point or more, and whose flags came or went, applications added or gone, and the decisions recorded between them. An application's Scores part shows its scores over time.
  • Shares of the portfolio. Reports → Shares of the portfolio lists the costliest tenth, the dearest tenth per user, the least used fifth, and the oldest fifth of the applications in service. A share never feeds a score.
  • Leadership reports. A manager makes a report for a period under Reports → Leadership reports: where the portfolio stood, how the scores moved between the period's snapshots, the decisions taken, the reviews closed, initiatives, requests, and retirements with their savings. It is worked out once and kept whole, with its SHA-256 digest checked whenever it is opened, and prints cleanly. A new guide, Insight, covers these.
  • As of a day. Reports → As of a day shows the applications in service at the end of a chosen day, with each one's stage and decision then beside now; an application's History part shows it as it stood on a day, value by value, with its decision and scores then. Both are read from the history: nothing is stored for them.
  • Scenarios. Portfolio → Scenarios holds proposed sets of changes (retire this, consolidate that into this, replace) with what each would do: the cost saved a year, the people whose application would change, the interfaces that would stop, the capabilities left with nothing, and the risk that would go with them. A scenario changes nothing until it is made into a proposed initiative.

Changed#

  • Three more rarely opened dialogs on a record's page (the people named on it, the capabilities it serves, and what it is called elsewhere) load when they are opened.

0.5.0 (2026-11-27)#

Wave 5, Planning change: decisions become planned, tracked change. A portfolio review draws its agenda from what needs deciding and keeps what it decided; a decision to change something becomes an initiative with its applications and dates, on a roadmap; a request for something new is checked against what already serves the need before anything is bought; an application cannot move to a later stage without what that stage requires; and a retirement is a plan with a checklist that ends with the application retired and its savings on record.

Upgrade with uv sync and uv run appwren db upgrade. Every row and file is kept, and no score moves. Stage gates are on after the upgrade: see Changed.

Added#

  • Portfolio reviews. Under Portfolio → Reviews, a manager or a unit's leader plans a review of a unit and everything beneath it, narrowed by tier if wished, with a date and a chair. Drawing the agenda adds the applications that need deciding: those whose decision differs from today's recommendation, whose decision is due for review by then, or that have none; more can be added by hand. Each item shows what was recommended and decided before, and a decision recorded while the review is open is shown on its item and kept there when it closes. A closed review changes no more. A review coming up is on Home for the people who run it.
  • Initiatives and the roadmap. An initiative is a planned change (a modernization, a migration, a consolidation, a replacement, a retirement, or another) with an owner, a stage, dates, and the applications it changes, retires, or moves work onto, each shown with the decision on record for it. Portfolio → Roadmap puts initiatives on a timeline by unit, kind, and stage, each bar a link. An application's page lists the initiatives that change it, and a decision to modernize, replace, consolidate, or retire offers to start one with its applications filled in. Their kinds and stages are lists the program can rename.
  • Requests for something new. Under Portfolio → Requests, anyone asks for something new: the need, the capability it serves, how many would use it, and when it is needed. The request shows every application that already serves that capability and any named like it. A steward of the unit, an architect, or a manager weighs options to build, buy, partner, or reuse what is on record, and ends it once, with a reason: approved, which adds an application in the proposed stage named for it; met by an application on record; or declined.
  • Stage gates. An application cannot move to a later stage without what that stage, and those before it, require; the editor, the API, an import, and a catalog file are refused with what is missing. Moving back, and retiring, need nothing. The gates are on by default and can be switched off on Admin → Program → Decisions, where a record still shows what it lacks.
  • The retirement playbook. On an application's new Retirement part, whoever keeps it starts a plan with a date (its planned retirement moves with it) and works through a checklist: its users told, its data archived and where, its interfaces removed, its licenses and contracts ended, access removed, and its infrastructure torn down, each step by a named person on a date with a note. With every step done, completing the plan retires the application and keeps its annual cost as the savings. Reports → Retirements lists those under way, those done, and the savings by year.
  • Planning change, a new guide, and the API page's routes for it.

Changed#

  • Home gives tasks, campaigns, and reviews icons of their own.
  • Three rarely opened dialogs on a record's page (what a component is built on, a technology's releases, and what a contract covers) load when they are opened.
  • Breaking: with the gates on by default, an import or an API call that moves an application to a later stage without what that stage requires is now refused. Switch the gates off to keep the old behaviour.

0.4.0 (2026-11-26)#

Wave 4, Signals: the facts other tools already know arrive by themselves, marked with where they came from, and never over a person. Connectors, each switched on by an administrator for one named source, read support dates, what the repositories know, and who signs in; the organization's own tools post the rest with a key that can do nothing else; a catalog file kept beside the code describes an application and its components. Engineering maturity is measured from checks rather than claims, and what sources supply joins the scores only where they supply it. A new installation still makes no connection to anything.

Upgrade with uv sync and uv run appwren db upgrade. Every row and file is kept, no connector is switched on, and no score moves until a source supplies something.

Added#

  • Connectors. Admin → Connectors lists the tools AppWren can read facts from. Each connector is added switched off, for one named source; it only reads; its credential stays in an environment variable named on the connector and is never stored. It can be checked before it is switched on, runs daily on the scheduler or when asked, and every run is recorded: what it read, what it changed, what it could not match, and why it failed. A run reads everything first, under a time limit and a size limit, and writes only if all of that succeeded, so a failed run changes nothing.
  • Values from other systems. A value a connector supplies is written to the record's own field, kept with its source and when it was read, marked as such on the record, and recorded in the history. A person who keeps the record can override it with a reason; the override wins until it is removed. Changing such a field by hand counts as an override.
  • End-of-life data. The first connector reads support dates for technology releases from the public endoflife.date dataset, for each technology with an end-of-life key, by the same rules as the import from a file: releases are added and dates updated, and nothing is removed.
  • Source code hosting. A connector for GitHub or a GitHub Enterprise server reads, for each component whose repository is in one organization: its languages, when its code last changed, how many people changed it over 90 days, its open dependency alerts by severity, and whether it has a README, tests, continuous integration, automated dependency updates, and a catalog file. An application whose every repository was read gets its maintainers from those people. The facts are shown on the component and on its application's Components part.
  • The identity provider. A connector for Microsoft Entra ID, read through Microsoft Graph, matches each application registered there to a record, by the provider's identifier kept on the record or by name or alias, and gives it its active users: the people who signed in over the last 30 days.
  • Not on record. Applications registered with the identity provider that match no record are listed once, on Portfolio → Not on record, for whoever adds applications: add one with its name filled in and its identifier kept, or dismiss it with a reason.
  • Push sources. An administrator issues a push key for one of the organization's own tools, shown once and kept only as a hash. The tool posts facts to POST /api/v1/facts in one format (a record, a field, a value, when it was observed), and can do nothing else. A batch is checked whole first, so one bad fact writes nothing. Incidents and support tickets over 90 days are fields that pushes fill.
  • Catalog files. An application and its components can be exported as a catalog-info.yaml to keep beside the code, and a file can be imported into an application, with a dry run first: it updates what it describes and adds any component it names, and removes nothing, so the same file twice changes nothing. Files are read with a safe loader; PyYAML is the sixth runtime dependency.
  • Engineering maturity. Automated checks for each component (monitoring, backups, documentation, tests, continuous integration, automated dependency updates, and no open critical dependency alert) give it bronze, silver, or gold, and its application the level of its least mature component, with what is missing for the next. They are shown on the component and on its application's Components part, as a filter of the applications list, and on Reports → Engineering maturity, by unit. The levels and their checks are in the scoring model, beside the cadence. Only what is built here, or has a repository, is measured.
  • Signals as factors. What sources supply joins the scores: incidents, open critical and high dependency alerts, code activity, and engineering maturity in technical health, and support tickets in cost and burden. Each counts only where a source supplies it, so an application without one scores as before, and the working names where each came from. An installation running an earlier default model unchanged gets them on upgrade.
  • Facts from other systems, a new guide, and a section on connector credentials in Configuration.
  • The history names the release whose dates changed, and shows the reason given for an override.

Changed#

  • A scoring model's dimension adds up to 100 without its signals; a signal comes on top.
  • The API fetches nothing an administrator has not switched on. A push key is the one credential besides a session, and it only posts facts.

Fixed#

  • A component's and a deployment's page named their application twice.

0.3.0 (2026-11-26)#

Wave 3, Keeping current: a portfolio that stays true without a central team chasing it. The app knows what falls due and says so, in the app and by email; owners confirm their records on a cadence, and a record nobody confirms lowers confidence where everyone can see it; campaigns ask the owners in a unit, by a date; departures, renewals, end of support, and grant ends raise tasks for whoever must act; anything on record can be found; and the program is shaped in the app, with a preview of what a change would do.

Upgrade with uv sync and uv run appwren db upgrade. Every row and file is kept, and no record is stale on the day of the upgrade.

Added#

  • The documentation site. The guides, with their pictures of the demo organization, are published at https://robert-mcdermott.github.io/appwren/: a home page, one page for each guide, the changelog, an API reference written from the OpenAPI document, search, and light and dark themes. node scripts/site/build.mjs builds it from docs/ with Node 22 and no packages, and fails on a broken link, anchor, or picture; node scripts/site/audit.mjs checks every page in both themes at desk and phone widths.
  • Scheduled work. The server runs jobs by itself: triggers, reminders, email, a monthly snapshot, and housekeeping. A process claims a job before running it, so several servers, or appwren jobs run from a scheduled task, run each one once between them. Admin → Scheduled work shows each job, when it last ran and what it did, and can run or pause it. [server] scheduler = false leaves the work to appwren jobs run.
  • Notices and reminders. A bell in the top bar counts what is new for you, and opens a page of it. Whoever something waits on is reminded two weeks before its date and the day after: an assessment about to expire, and a decision's review date and target date. An assessment or a target that stays late goes to the unit's steward after a week and its leader after three. Each is said once for a given date.
  • Email, optional. Name an SMTP server under [mail] and notices go by email as well, each as it happens or in a daily summary, as each person chooses under Your account. The password comes from the environment. Until email is set up nothing is attempted, and nothing written before then is ever sent. appwren mail test sends a test message, and appwren doctor checks the server.
  • Confirming a record. The people named on an application, its unit's stewards, and managers confirm that its record is right, in one step from its page: every 6 months for tiers 1 and 2, every 12 for the rest. A record past its confirmation is stale: its page says so, it is flagged, data quality and the portfolio list count it, its owner is reminded, and its data confidence is 15 points lower until someone confirms it. A record never confirmed is due one period after it was added or after the upgrade, whichever is later, so nothing is stale on the day of the upgrade.
  • Campaigns. Managers and stewards ask the owners of the applications in a unit, narrowed by tier and stage if they wish, to confirm their records or to assess their applications by a date (CMG-0002). Before starting, it says how many people it would ask and which applications have nobody to ask. Each person is told once, with a count, and finds what is asked of them on Home. An item closes itself when the record is confirmed or the application assessed after the campaign began. The campaign's page shows its progress and its coverage by unit, least covered first, and can remind, excuse an application with a reason, close, or cancel.
  • Tasks, raised by what happens. Each hour the triggers look at the dates and the people on record. A contract's notice date within 60 days, a release in use within 180 days of its end of support, a grant within 180 days of its end, and an accountable owner who has left each raise a task for the person who must act, with a date and the reason, and tell them. A cause raises one task, never two. A task closes by itself when its cause is settled (the contract renewed, nothing on the release, a decision recorded, someone named) or by a person with a note. Tasks are on Home, on a page of their own, and in the reminders; the reminder of a contract's notice date is now its task's.
  • Passing on what someone holds. Every person has a page of what they hold: the records they are named on and in what role, the contracts and interfaces they own, their open tasks, and what campaigns still ask of them. An administrator or a manager passes all of it to someone else in one step. Assessments and decisions stay with whoever made them.
  • One search. The command palette finds records as well as pages, and Search lists every kind of record that matches, the best answer first: by name, reference, summary, or what something is called elsewhere. It finds only what the searcher may open.
  • Saved views. Any list's filters are kept under a name from its Views button, privately, or shared with everyone by stewards, architects, and managers. A view keeps the question, so each person who opens it sees only what they may.
  • Applications by what they are built on. The applications and components lists, and their exports, narrow to a technology, one of its releases, or an exact version, such as every application on .NET 6, or on 6.0.25. A technology's page links each release to that list. An application counts when any of its components is on it.
  • Likely duplicates. Adding or renaming an application shows those already on record whose name or alias is close, or that are the same product from the same vendor.
  • Contesting a score. Whoever keeps an application says a factor is wrong, what it should be, and why, with a link to the evidence. A manager or an architect accepts or answers it, and the owner is told. Both stay on the record and in its history. A contest does not move a score; putting the record or the assessment right does.
  • The program, shaped in the app. Admin → Program, for managers of the whole organization and administrators: rename, describe, reorder, add to, and retire the values of a list (those AppWren's rules read can be renamed and reordered only); add and edit the organization's own fields and say which are sensitive; reword the questions and their levels; and change the thresholds, every profile's weights, and the cadence. A changed scoring model is previewed first, listing every application whose scores, confidence, recommendation, or flags would differ, and publishing it changes exactly those. Each change publishes a new version or is kept in the history.
  • A portfolio snapshot each month, kept by the scheduler on the first turn of the month.
  • Starting with one team. appwren init asks for the organization's name and a first unit within it, such as the team starting with AppWren (--organization and --first-unit when unattended), so an application can be added at once. Admin → Units → Put a unit above the top puts a new top unit above the existing one, for a team that began at the top. Getting started has a section on starting with one team.
  • Owners deciding about their own applications, if switched on. A program setting, off by default, on Admin → Program → Decisions: an application's accountable owner who is also a manager or a leader over it may record its decision, for a team too small for anyone else to. Each such decision is marked Recorded by its own owner on the decision, in the application's history, and on the Decisions page.
  • Guides: a new guide, Keeping current; shaping the program in the app, scheduled work, and email in Running the program and Configuration; what a stale record costs in The portfolio; the new routes in the API guide; and pictures of the new screens.

Changed#

  • The default scoring model gains the cadence of wave 3: how often records are confirmed and what a stale one costs, when reminders go, and how far ahead tasks are raised. An upgrade brings it to a model that nobody has changed since 0.1.0 or 0.2.0; one the organization has shaped is left alone and uses the same values until it says otherwise.

Fixed#

  • A bubble on the portfolio chart opened a page that was not there. Links inside a drawing were followed by the wrong address. Clicking a bubble now opens its application, and hovering one shows which application it is and where it stands. Present since 0.1.0.
  • The demo's research software engineer was named on no application, though the guides sent people to see the applications they keep. They now keep the two pieces of research software in the demo's storylines.

0.2.0 (2026-11-26)#

Wave 2, The landscape: each application in its surroundings. What it sends and receives and what data passes, what depends on it and what breaks if it goes, where it runs, what it costs and what pays, the contracts behind it, what else does the same job, and what it is built on that is falling out of support.

Upgrade with uv sync and uv run appwren db upgrade. Every row and file is kept.

Added#

  • Interfaces. Data passing from one application to another is a record of its own, with a reference (INT-0007), a method, how often, the classes of data it carries, and an owner. An application's new Connections part shows what it sends and receives. An interface is seen by whoever may see both of its applications and kept by whoever keeps either.
  • Data flows. Landscape → Data flows follows a class of data between applications, and marks any interface that delivers a class the receiving application's record does not list.
  • How a record is connected. Every application, component, capability, technology, vendor, and interface opens a picture of what it is joined to, with any neighbor opened a ring further. It works from the keyboard, and a list beside it says everything the picture does.
  • What breaks if this goes. An application's Connections part lists what depends on it, directly and through those; the interfaces that would stop; the capabilities left with no provider, or without their primary; and the people and units affected.
  • Hosting platforms and deployments. Landscape → Hosting holds the data centers, cloud accounts, clusters, and providers things run on. An application's Components part says where it, or any of its components, runs: which platform, in which environment, at what address. A platform's page lists everything on it, which is what would stop with it.
  • Cost lines and funding. An application's new Cost part breaks its yearly cost into lines by what each is for and what pays for it. Once it has lines, its annual cost is their sum. Funding sources (budgets, grants, chargebacks, capital projects) are records of their own, each showing what it pays for.
  • Contracts. Portfolio → Contracts holds agreements with vendors: the applications each covers and the licenses they use, the term, the date by which the vendor must be told, and whether a data agreement is in place. A contract is shown only to those who may see the cost of an application it covers.
  • Two cost factors and two flags. Cost and burden now weighs cost per user, and the share of licenses bought that are in use. Flags are raised for a contract's notice date within 90 days, and for confidential or restricted data under a contract with no data agreement.
  • Cost on the dashboard, by category and by what pays, for those who see every application's cost.
  • Overlap. Landscape → Overlap lists every capability served by more than one application in service, with those applications side by side: scores, recommendation, decision, people, and cost where the reader may see it.
  • The capability map, measured. The map can show, for each capability, how many applications serve it, the average of any score, data confidence, or the technical health of the weakest application that serves it. Each figure opens the applications behind it.
  • Lifecycle. Landscape → Lifecycle shows the technology releases applications run on, soonest end of support first, as a timeline with how many components and applications are on each; and the applications with a planned retirement.
  • Radar. The technologies in use by ring (adopt, trial, assess, hold), with how much runs on each, and those in use that nobody has placed.
  • Support dates from a file. appwren technologies import-eol FILE, or Import support dates on the Technologies page, adds releases and updates their dates from a file of end-of-life data, matched by each technology's end-of-life key. It says what it would change before it changes anything, and removes nothing. AppWren does not fetch the file.
  • Data handling. Reports → Data handling lists the applications that handle a chosen class of data, with who answers for each, where it runs, how people sign in, what it is built on and whether that is supported, the interfaces that carry the class, and whether a data agreement covers it. The same rows export as CSV or JSON.
  • History, in one place. Reports → History shows every change, newest first, to managers, auditors, and administrators: narrowed to a person, a record, a kind of record, or dates.
  • Links to other systems. An identifier in another system is shown as a link where the program gives that system an address pattern, such as https://code.example.org/{id}.
  • Lists of applications, the portfolio list, and their exports can be narrowed by capability and by class of data.
  • Guides. A new guide, The landscape; cost and contracts in The portfolio; and what an upgrade does to the program in Running the program.

Changed#

  • An application that receives data through an interface now counts among the dependents of the application that sends it, so recording interfaces can raise a source's business value.
  • The default scoring model has a new version. Cost and burden is now annual cost 30, cost per user 15, license use 10, support effort 25, and change effort 20, so scores for cost and burden move. An installation still running the previous default gets the new one on upgrade; one that has shaped its model keeps it, and can take the new factors from the default program when it chooses.
  • A record's history names the vendor, application, or person a change refers to, where it showed a number.

Fixed#

  • A record's history told an assessment, a decision, a capability served, and a unit that uses an application each as "Added this application". Each is now told for what it is, and the copy of a decision onto its application is no longer told as a second change.

0.1.0 (2026-11-26)#

Wave 1, The portfolio: install, sign in, and keep one record of every application, with what it is built on and what it does for the organization; assess it, read four scores with their working, see the recommendation and its rule, and record what people decided.

Added#

  • Foundation. appwren init, serve, db upgrade, db current, verify, and doctor. Deployment settings in appwren.toml with environment overrides; secrets are refused in the file. SQLite by default and PostgreSQL by address, on one model and one set of migrations. Every change is written to an append-only, hash-chained history in the same transaction, and appwren verify finds the first event that has been altered.
  • Accounts and roles. Local accounts with scrypt-hashed passwords, server-side sessions, CSRF protection, and throttled sign-in. Seven roles: admin, manager, architect, auditor, leader, steward, and member, some limited to a unit and everything beneath it. Every API route is checked against one policy table.
  • Single sign-on. Optional OpenID Connect beside local accounts, with the provider's application roles or groups mapped to roles, unit scopes, and memberships under Admin.
  • Units. The organization as a hierarchy of any depth, with CSV import of units and people that can be run dry first and reports every problem by row.
  • The web app. Sign-in, the app shell with a command palette (⌘K), your account, and admin screens for people, units, single sign-on, and appearance. Light, dark, and system modes and three palettes, each checked for contrast. Branding with a live preview.
  • Applications. Each with a permanent reference (APP-0042), the unit that answers for it, a lifecycle stage and its date, sourcing, purpose, pace, domain, criticality tier, vendor and product, hosting, users, maintainers, sign-in, data handled, monitoring, backup, cost and funding, research facts, and the organization's own fields. Exactly one accountable owner, and people in other roles. The units that use it. What its stage still requires, shown on the record.
  • Components, technologies, vendors, and capabilities. Components with the technologies and releases they are built on; technologies with releases and their support dates, and whether each is supported, ending, or past its end; vendors; a capability map, loaded with a starter map, with at most one primary provider per capability. Applications depend on, are part of, and replace one another.
  • Sensitive fields. Cost and security details are withheld from people with no role over an application, in records, lists, filters, sorting, and history.
  • Program packs. appwren program export and appwren program import move the whole program between installations, checked whole before anything is loaded, with a dry run.
  • Assessment. A short question bank, each question with a definition for every level and Don't know always allowed. Assessments are never edited; the next supersedes the last, and answers expire after 12 to 24 months depending on the tier.
  • Scores with their working. Business value, technical health, cost and burden, and risk exposure, each a weighted average of defined factors from answers, recorded facts, and what the records around the application say (technology support, capabilities, dependents). Data confidence says how much is known. Every score opens to its factors, levels, weights, and the sum.
  • Recommendations and flags. Ordered rules recommend invest, maintain, tolerate, modernize, replace, consolidate, retire, incubate, or assess, with guardrails; flags such as restricted data on unsupported technology, a grant ending, or one person keeping it. Profiles for shared platforms and research software. The scoring model and question bank are versioned, and a snapshot keeps a score as it was.
  • Decisions. A manager, or a leader of the application's unit, records a disposition with a rationale, a review date, a target date, and a successor; never the application's own accountable owner. Each decision keeps the scores it was made against, and is never edited. Where today's recommendation differs, both are shown as a mismatch.
  • The portfolio chart. Business value against technical health, or against cost and burden; bubbles sized by cost where the reader may see it and colored by risk exposure; thresholds and quadrants; a table of the same points; and the applications too little is known about to place.
  • Portfolio snapshots. A manager keeps every application's scores together for a review; each snapshot reopens unchanged.
  • Home. One list of what each person owes, most urgent first: applications to assess, flags, and records missing what their stage requires; for stewards, applications nobody answers for; for managers and leaders, decisions waiting.
  • The dashboard and data quality. Where the portfolio stands by recommendation, decision, lifecycle, sourcing, flags, confidence, and technology support; and what is missing, by unit. Every figure opens exactly the list it counts.
  • Import and export. Applications come in from CSV with a dry run that names every problem by row, and update by reference; nothing is loaded unless every row is good. Every list exports as CSV or JSON, withholding what the reader may not see, and each export is recorded.
  • The demo organization. appwren demo builds the fictional Bramblewood Institute for Health Research, with two years of history made by the real services under a simulated clock: units, people, applications and their parts, assessments, quarterly snapshots, and decisions, with storylines for every screen. It marks itself as a demo, offers one-click sign-in as its sample people from the machine it runs on, and refuses to listen beyond it unless started with --demo-on-network.
  • Guides. Getting started, the portfolio, running the program, configuration, and the API, with screenshots of the demo organization taken by scripts/capture.mjs; the command reference and the OpenAPI document are written from the code, and a test keeps them current.
  • Accessibility checks. scripts/a11y-audit.mjs builds a fresh demo and checks 38 screens in three palettes, light and dark, for names, labels, headings, landmarks, target sizes, and the contrast of every piece of text. scripts/keyboard-check.mjs checks the skip link, a record's parts, the working behind a score, and the portfolio chart from the keyboard. Neither needs anything installed beyond Node and Chrome.
